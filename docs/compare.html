How this site compares with FDA Drug Trials Snapshots

The FDA already publishes who took part in the trials behind new drugs. Here is what that covers, and what this site adds.

Based on the FDA's Snapshots pages and reports, checked November 6, 2026. Sources are listed below.

What Snapshots do well

Each Snapshot is a plain-language page about one new drug. It reports the sex, race, ethnicity, and age of the people in the key trials, where the trials were run, and whether benefits or side effects differed between groups.

What they do not cover

Snapshots cover only newly approved drugs, and they reflect what was known at approval. The FDA also says they should not be the only thing used to make decisions about medical care.

What this site adds

It looks at a condition instead of a single drug. It compares participants with the people who have the condition, and it shows how often trials do not report who took part.

What this site does not do

We do not rate trials or give medical advice. The patient comparison covers breast and prostate cancer so far, and our data includes only trials that posted results.

Other efforts

  • ICER's Clinical trial Diversity Rating. A framework that gives a trial an overall diversity rating. People download a template to use it. This site does not rate trials.
  • GSK's study of its own trials. GSK compared 495 of its US trials (108,261 participants, 2002 to 2019) with both US Census figures and disease data. The answer changed with the benchmark. In HIV trials, Black participants were 35.1% of enrollment, compared with 13.4% of the Census and 55.3% of people with HIV.
  • Research that uses Snapshots data. A 2025 study of 341 trials behind drugs approved from 2017 to 2023 compared participants with the Census. It notes that Snapshots give only overall demographics, not the demographics of US sites.
