Who Is in the Trial?

This site shows who took part in clinical studies for four common conditions, and how often those studies reported participants' race. It uses records from ClinicalTrials.gov for interventional studies, which assign participants to a treatment or other intervention, that started in 2010 or later and posted results. Most records list a study phase, a stage in testing a drug. Others list no phase, such as screening, behavioral, or device studies, and the record type filter lets you include or leave them out. We don't rate any trial. Explore the numbers and draw your own conclusions.

Trial participants compared with patients (PPR)

PPR is a group's share of trial participants divided by its share of people diagnosed with the condition. The dashed line marks 1.0, where the two shares are equal. The hollow marker shows the same value with the single trial that contributed the most participants to that group left out, so you can see how much one trial moves the result. It uses every record with all sites in the US, whatever the record type and phase filters are set to, so those two filters do not change it.

Sources: trial shares from the ClinicalTrials.gov API, using trials with all sites in the US. Patient shares from NCI SEER*Explorer, new cases from 2019 to 2023. Method: build_ppr.py.

How to read this

What is a study phase?

Treatments are tested in stages. Phase 1 trials are small safety tests, Phase 2 checks whether the treatment works and at what dose, Phase 3 compares it with existing care in large groups, and Phase 4 follows it after approval.

What is PPR?

The Participation to Prevalence Ratio compares a group's share of trial participants with its share of people who have the condition. A value of 1.0 means the two shares are equal, and "underrepresented" means a value below 1. Each value is shown next to a reference line at 1.0, and individual groups are not labeled. PPR uses only trials with all sites in the US and leaves participants with unknown race or ethnicity out of the shares. Patient data comes from the National Cancer Institute's SEER program. Data from the CDC for type 2 diabetes and heart failure is being added.

Limits of this data

We include interventional trials started in 2010 or later that posted results on ClinicalTrials.gov. About a quarter of the records list no study phase. Many of these are screening, behavioral, or device studies, and some are very large, so they can move the totals a lot. Use the record type filter to see the numbers with or without them. The trial data refreshes every day, and numbers change as trials post new results, so figures in our reflections and presentation reflect the snapshot date we cite. Trials that used their own race categories cannot be combined with the standard categories, so they are counted as reporting race but left out of the race chart. Trials listed under more than one condition count fully in each condition's numbers, so adding conditions together would count them twice. For PPR, prostate cancer leaves out trials that are also listed under breast cancer. SEER counts new cases, not everyone living with a condition. SEER's Asian or Pacific Islander and American Indian or Alaska Native groups leave out Hispanic patients, while the trial groups include them, so those comparisons are approximate.

Sources and methods

  • Trial data: the ClinicalTrials.gov API, a public database of clinical studies. We include interventional studies, which assign participants to a treatment or other intervention, that started in 2010 or later and posted results. Each number comes from a study's baseline table, using the column for all participants.
  • Patient data: NCI SEER*Explorer from the National Cancer Institute, new cases of breast and prostate cancer from 2019 to 2023.
