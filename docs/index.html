Who Is in the Trial?

Clinical trials decide which treatments reach the public. This site shows who actually took part in trials for four common conditions, and how often those trials reported participants' race. We don't rate any trial. Explore the numbers and draw your own conclusions.

How to read this

What is a study phase?

Treatments are tested in stages. Phase 1 trials are small safety tests, Phase 2 checks whether the treatment works and at what dose, Phase 3 compares it with existing care in large groups, and Phase 4 follows it after approval.

What is PPR?

The Participation to Prevalence Ratio compares a group's share of trial participants with its share of people who have the condition. A value of 1.0 means the two shares are equal, and "underrepresented" means a value below 1. Each value is shown next to a reference line at 1.0, and individual groups are not labeled. PPR uses only trials with all sites in the US and leaves participants with unknown race or ethnicity out of the shares. Patient data comes from the National Cancer Institute's SEER program. Data from the CDC for type 2 diabetes and heart failure is being added.

Limits of this data

We include interventional trials started in 2010 or later that posted results on ClinicalTrials.gov. Trials that used their own race categories cannot be combined with the standard categories, so they are counted as reporting race but left out of the race chart. Trials listed under more than one condition count fully in each condition's numbers, so adding conditions together would count them twice. For PPR, prostate cancer leaves out trials that are also listed under breast cancer. SEER counts new cases, not everyone living with a condition. SEER's Asian or Pacific Islander and American Indian or Alaska Native groups leave out Hispanic patients, while the trial groups include them, so those comparisons are approximate.
